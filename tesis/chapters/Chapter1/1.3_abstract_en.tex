The AI4Mars dataset provides pixel-level segmentation masks, produced by crowdsourced
annotation, for tens of thousands of images taken on the surface of Mars. These masks have been
used almost exclusively to train neural networks. This work asks which quantitative indicators of
rock coverage and organisation can be derived from them reproducibly, and what the limits of
their validity are with respect to differences in annotation and to human judgement.

A classical image-processing procedure was implemented. For each mask it computes visible rock
coverage as the ratio of rock pixels to labelled pixels, and estimates the number of individual
rocks through connected components, the distance transform and a watershed guided by prominence
maxima, with explicit area and shape filters. It was applied to the \cNEscenas{} images from the
\textit{Curiosity} rover navigation camera that have a training mask.

Coverage could be computed for \cNCobCalc{} scenes (\cPctCobCalc); \cNCobPos{} (\cPctCobPos{}
of the total) had coverage greater than zero, with a median of \cCobMedVal{} over labelled
pixels, and no evidence was found that high values are explained by the labelled fraction of the
scene. The count, applied to the \cFlagOk{} eligible scenes, yielded \cNRocas{} rocks. The two
indicators show practically no linear association ($r = \cDepCCPearson$), although non-linear
measures detect a weak dependence. In the \cNGold{} expert masks included in the dataset, which
cover other images, the median coverage of scenes with rock is \cGoldCobMedPos. In a
descriptive decomposition based on a trained segmenter, used as a common instrument on a sample
from each source, approximately \cDescImgPct{} of the difference in mean coverage is assigned to
differences between the image samples —the expert scenes, sampled in a balanced way across
terrain units, are far less rocky— and the remaining \cDescAnotPct{}, about two percentage
points, to the annotation source. This proportion is conditional on the model used and is not a
causal estimate, and the interval of the annotation component includes zero once the grouping
of images by sol is taken into account.
An exploratory evaluation with one observer, who counted the rocks within the region annotated
as big rock, suggests systematic undercounting: the procedure falls below the observer in
\cVAbajo{} of \cVN{} scenes and above in \cVArriba{} (weighted kappa \cVKappaPond). It
reproducibly counts the geometrically separable instances within that class, but not the number
of visible rocks: a single polygon often groups several blocks, and the class covers only part
of the rock in the scene.

The result is therefore negative for counting: counting individual rocks from these masks is not
straightforward. It is, instead, diagnostic for the dataset. Previous work had already pointed
to ambiguity and annotation problems in AI4Mars; this work quantifies how its properties —expert
scenes of very different composition by sampling design, a small and uncertain difference in
annotation criteria, a big-rock class with low agreement among experts, and partial annotation
of blocks— affect the coverage and count indicators. The work also delivers a results set with one row per image and
twenty-nine columns, and an evaluation design that descriptively decomposes the difference between
sources into a part assigned to the images and a part assigned to the annotation.
