The AI4Mars masks indicate, pixel by pixel, the type of terrain in tens of thousands of images
of the surface of Mars, and they have almost always been used to train neural networks. This
work uses them differently: as a map of the terrain from which indicators can be measured
directly, without training any model, and it examines how far those indicators are valid.

For the \cNEscenas{} images from the \textit{Curiosity} navigation camera, three indicators were
computed: rock coverage, that is, the share of the labelled area that is rock; the composition
of the terrain; and, as an approximation to the number of rocks, the number of separable regions
in the areas marked as big rock. They were
then compared with the expert masks included in the dataset, with a manual count by the author
and with a trained model.

Coverage was computed reproducibly for \cPctCobCalc{} of the images, and no evidence was found
that it depends on how much of the scene was labelled, although it cannot be checked against an
independent measurement of the terrain. The rock count, by contrast, did not reach its
objective: its agreement with the author's manual count is slight, it returns zero in one in five
eligible scenes, and it tends to undercount. In the first round of the manual count it found
fewer rocks than the author in \cVAbajo{} of the \cVDesac{} scenes where they disagreed; in a repetition of that
count the tendency remained, although it was not statistically significant. Both the annotation,
which often draws several rocks as a single region and marks only some of them, and the tuning
of the procedure itself contribute to this. The difference from the expert masks is mostly due to
the experts having annotated far less rocky images; whether the way of annotating also plays a
part could not be determined.

The work delivers a characterisation of the terrain with its limits measured, a procedure that
anyone can repeat, and a diagnosis of what can be expected from AI4Mars for measuring terrain.
