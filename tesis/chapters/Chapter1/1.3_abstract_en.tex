\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Mars surface missions have accumulated hundreds of thousands of navigation-camera images,
and the AI4Mars dataset additionally provides pixel-level segmentation masks produced by
crowdsourced annotation. These masks have been used almost exclusively to train neural
networks, so the quantitative information they already encode remains largely untapped.
This work asks which terrain indicators can be derived directly from them without
training any model.

A reproducible classical image-processing procedure was implemented. For each mask it
computes visible rock coverage as the ratio of rock pixels to labelled pixels, and
estimates the number of individual rocks through connected components, the Euclidean
distance transform and a watershed guided by prominence maxima, with explicit area and
shape filters. The procedure was run over the \num{16064} images from the
\textit{Curiosity} rover navigation camera that have a training mask available.

Coverage proved computable for \SI{67.3}{\percent} of the scenes, with a median of
\SI{96.8}{\percent} over labelled pixels, while the count applied to \num{2193} images
and yielded \num{4204} rocks with a decreasing size--frequency distribution. The two
indicators turned out to be statistically independent ($r = 0.02$), confirming that they
measure distinct facets of the terrain. Validation against the \num{322} expert masks
included in the dataset itself revealed that crowdsourced annotation systematically
overestimates coverage —the median drops to \SI{46.1}{\percent}— a bias attributed to the
greater visual salience of rock compared with soil and sand. An error-mode diagnosis
showed that the ceiling on counting lies not in image resolution but in label taxonomy:
the \textit{big rock} class contributes only \SI{2.5}{\percent} of annotated rock and is
even scarcer in the expert masks.

The work delivers a results set with twenty-four per-image indicators, a quantitative
diagnosis of the dataset's limitations, and a comparison against two machine-learning
approaches that places the classical method's performance in context. As an applied
An additional test of the trained segmenter against expert masks shows that, despite
having learnt from biased annotations, it estimates coverage without appreciable bias,
suggesting the indicator could be computed without human annotation. As an applied
extension, the indicators were translated into six terrain-risk alert rules with explicit,
justified thresholds, and into a desktop application that makes the results consultable. A
negative result is also documented: detecting mineral veins from the imagery is not
feasible with this material, for instrumental reasons that are specified.
