The AI4Mars masks indicate, pixel by pixel, the type of terrain in tens of thousands of images
of the surface of Mars, and they have almost always been used to train neural networks. This
work uses them differently: as a map of the terrain from which indicators can be measured
directly, without training any model, and it examines how far those indicators are valid.

For the \cNEscenas{} images from the \textit{Curiosity} navigation camera, three indicators were
computed: rock coverage, that is, the share of the labelled area that is rock; the composition
of the terrain; and the number of individual rocks in the areas marked as big rock. They were
then compared with the expert masks included in the dataset, with a manual count by the author
and with a trained model.

Coverage was computed reproducibly for \cPctCobCalc{} of the images, and no evidence was found
that it depends on how much of the scene was labelled. The rock count, by contrast, did not
reach its objective: its agreement with the manual count is modest, it tends to undercount, and
it returns zero in one in five eligible scenes, because the annotation often draws several rocks
as a single region and marks only some of them. The difference from the expert masks is mostly
due to the experts having annotated far less rocky images, and only to a small extent to the way
of annotating.

The work delivers a characterisation of the terrain with its limits measured, a procedure that
anyone can repeat, and a diagnosis of what can be expected from AI4Mars for measuring terrain.
